Service design · Information architecture · ITI · 2025 – 2026

One place everyone checks first

When I joined the Game Development Academy at ITI, nobody handed anything over to me. Files, rules and contacts were all over the place, so I built the dashboards I wish I'd had on day one.

Students Dashboarddeadline · weekly checkpointfor students Staff Dashboardonboarding guide · who is whofor staff "where's the schedule??" "what are the rules?"
The problem

No handover, and files, rules and contacts were scattered everywhere.

What I did

Asked staff and students what they needed, then built a staff and a student dashboard.

The result

~7 staff and all students used them, and most repeat questions stopped.

RoleSolo: research, IA, build
Users~7 staff, every student
MethodsUser questions, repeat-question log, iteration
ToolsGoogle Sheets, Forms, Drive
01Problem

Learning everything from scratch

I had to learn everything from scratch. Course plans, schedules, rules and contacts were scattered across drives, chats and inboxes. I spent hours hunting for files, other staff did too, and students kept asking me the same questions because there was nowhere to look.

02Research

The questions I kept hearing

I asked staff and students what they looked for most, and paid attention to the questions that landed on my desk again and again. Those became my content plan.

"What are the rules we need to follow?"Students, often
"Where's the schedule for my track?"Students, every week
"Where can I find my grades?"Students
"What's the login for the academy account?"Staff and students
"When is the next deadline or event?"Students
"Who do I contact about this?"Staff
03Synthesis

Two audiences, two different jobs

When I grouped the questions, it was obvious that staff and students needed completely different things. One dashboard for everyone would have been a mess, so I made two.

Staff · ~7 people

"Help me run the track"

  • Step-by-step onboarding for new instructors
  • End-of-course wrap-up and weekly setup checklists
  • Who is who: who to contact for what
  • Track plan, course list and instructor directory
  • File templates and important links
Students · every student in the tracks

"Tell me where things are"

  • Rules, FAQ and the student handbook
  • Schedules for each track
  • Submission folders and the weekly checkpoint
  • Course feedback forms
  • Upcoming deadlines, events and game jams
  • Learning resources and self-study courses
04Design

Have a look around

Staff DashboardGame Development Academy · placeholder data
FileEditViewInsertFormatDataTools
fxDashboard

The real sheets, with names, contacts and links replaced by placeholders and long notes shortened. The tour switches between pages on its own; pick a tab or press pause to look around.

Single source of truthEnter it once, use it everywhere

The track plan looks up each instructor's email from the instructor directory, so contact details are only updated in one place.

Progress at a glanceHours and status calculate themselves

The course list works out lecture, lab and self-study hours from credit hours, and shows how much of the track is done, in progress or not started.

05Iteration

It kept changing as people used it

  1. Launch. Replaced the old dashboard with the new structure for staff and students.
  2. Listen. Staff and students told me what was missing or hard to find.
  3. Update. I added or moved content after each round of feedback, so the dashboards kept matching what people actually needed.
06Impact

Fewer questions, faster work

~7staff using the staff dashboard
Allstudents on the student dashboard
Mostrepeat questions stopped coming
Fastertasks finished, with time saved

Staff and students told me it made their lives so much easier: files, answers, deadlines and events all in one place. My favourite part was the quiet week when the same old questions just stopped. It also fed my feedback loop, since the course feedback forms and weekly checkpoints lived right on the student dashboard.

Next iteration

If I did it again

Measure itPut numbers on the time saved

Count repeat questions per week, and time a few common tasks like "find this week's schedule", before and after each change.

Tree testingCheck the categories with users

Ask new students and staff to find specific items using only the tab and section names, to see which labels still cause confusion.

Security & AISafer logins, and answers on demand
  • Move shared account logins out of the spreadsheet into a password manager with proper access control.
  • Add an assistant that answers common questions from the handbook and rules, links to the source, and hands anything else to staff.

42 students, one feedback loop

As Game Art Track Supervisor at ITI, I wanted to know early if students weren't getting what they came for, and fix it while it still mattered to them.

Course feedbackHow useful was this course?★★★★☆How was the instructor?★★★★★Anything we should change?after every course Weekly checkpointTask 1 submittedTask 2 submittedTask 3 in progressevery week 1:1 every month ♡ 42 students
The problem

Course problems only showed up after students had already lost out.

What I did

Weekly check-ins, monthly 1:1s and surveys after every course, tracked in sheets.

The result

I adjusted the curriculum, and students said it really helped them.

RoleSupervisor & researcher
Participants~42 game development students
MethodsCheck-ins, 1:1s, surveys
ToolsOnline forms, spreadsheets
01Problem

Finding out too late

A track has lots of courses, each with a different instructor. If you only hear from students at the end, a bad course has already cost that whole group weeks.

02Method

How I kept listening

  1. Every weekCheckpoint meetingsShort meetings with each student to follow their tasks and catch anyone falling behind.
  2. Every month1:1 sessionsConversations about their experience of the track, plus feedback on their performance.
  3. After each courseSurveysQuestions on the course content and the instructor.
  4. OngoingTracking sheetsSurvey results organised per instructor so patterns across courses stand out.
  5. OutcomeChangesAdjust the curriculum or the instructor when students aren't benefiting.
…and it starts again with the next course ↺
03Journey

Where it fit in a student's week

StudentWorks on assigned tasksLearns with the course instructorFinishes the courseReflects on their progressStarts the next course
ListeningCheckpoint meetingCheckpoints continueCourse & instructor survey1:1 sessionTracking sheets reviewed
SurfacesWho is falling behindProblems while they happenHow useful the course and teaching wereThe student's overall experiencePatterns per instructor

04Impact

What changed

42students followed week by week
Everycourse evaluated by its students
Per instructorperformance tracked over time

I adjusted the curriculum based on what students told me, and the survey sheets helped me spot when a course or an instructor needed a change. A lot of students said it really helped them, and honestly, that meant a lot to me.

05Takeaways

What I learned about myself

  • Quick weekly check-ins catch problems early, 1:1s tell you why, and surveys let you compare. I like using all three.
  • I enjoy turning a pile of messy answers into something people can actually make decisions with.
  • Listening only counts if something changes, and the next round of feedback tells you if it worked.
Next iteration

What I'd add next time

Thematic analysisCode the open answers, not just the scores

Tag every written comment, group the tags with affinity mapping, and count how often each theme appears, so recurring issues are visible even when ratings look fine.

Reducing biasOffer an anonymous route

Students may soften criticism of an instructor they will see again. An anonymous survey option makes honest answers safer to give.

Service blueprintMap what happens behind each touchpoint

Add the instructor and admin steps behind every student touchpoint, to find where a fix actually needs to happen.

Measuring changeCompare before and after

Track the same survey questions before and after a curriculum change, so each change can show whether it helped.

Questbook: a life notebook that plays like a game

I work in games and I'm a bit obsessed with productivity. I kept forgetting tasks and dropping planners after a week, so I built a planner you come back to because it's fun, not because you have to.

The problem

I kept forgetting plans and dropping planners after a week.

What I did

Designed and built a planner that works like a game, using the four laws of habits.

The result

I use it every day, and it's growing into a product I plan to sell.

RoleSolo: research, UX, UI, prototype
PlatformWeb app, desktop & mobile
Built withAI-assisted prototyping
StatusIn progress, planned for sale
01Why

The idea I couldn't let go of

Working in games showed me how easily a good game keeps you coming back: you always know your next goal, you can see yourself getting better, and small wins feel great. Then I read self-help books like Atomic Habits and kept thinking, why does every planner feel like homework? I'd start one, forget my plans by Wednesday, and stop opening it. I've had this idea in my head for a long time, and Questbook is me finally building it.

02Market gap

What was missing in the apps I tried

I tried a lot of productivity, habit and journaling apps. Most had very few features, I needed a different app for each part of my life, and none of them used the four laws of habits to make sticking with things easier.

Typical productivity appQuestbook
Habits, goals and journalUsually separate appsOne notebook
Four laws of behaviour changeNot built inBuilt into every habit
MotivationStreaks and remindersQuests, XP, coins, levels and rewards
Forgotten tasksA list you stop openingToday pulls in plans, deadlines and due habits
KidsI couldn't find anyA kids therapy section, guided by parents
Busy daysA broken streak2-minute versions and pause modes
PersonalisationLimitedYour own areas, rewards, avatar and sections
03Hypothesis

If I make journaling and habit tracking fun, like a game, people will actually stick with them.

04UX approach

Turning the four laws into things you can tap

Every law became real features on the screen, so the app quietly does the hard part for you.

1Make it obvious
  • Habit stacks: "After I…, I will…"
  • A time and place for every habit
  • Today shows only what's due, with clear cues
2Make it attractive
  • Goals become illustrated quest cards
  • Temptation bundling: pair a habit with something you enjoy
  • A customisable avatar, stickers and seasonal events
3Make it easy
  • A 2-minute version of every habit
  • "Take it easy" modes for low-energy, sick and travel days
  • "One thing" mode when everything feels like too much
4Make it satisfying
  • XP, coins and levels for every action
  • A reward shop with rewards you choose yourself
  • Progress you can see: trackers, the "1% better" curve, milestones
IdentityEvery action is a vote

Habits are linked to who you want to become, and the app shows which identity each one supports.

Breaking habitsThe laws, inverted

Bad habits get the opposite treatment: make it invisible, unattractive, hard and unsatisfying, with a days-clean counter.

CustomisedBuilt around the player

Pick your life areas, rewards, milestones and avatar, and hide any Today section you don't need.

05Problem solving

Things that went wrong, and how I fixed them

I use Questbook every day, so I felt every annoying thing myself, and the UX feedback round caught even more. My rule for every fix: keep it fun, and make it easy to come back.

Confusing currency

I had XP and coins. Even I got confused, and spending felt like losing progress.

One wallet: every XP earned also lands in your wallet, and spending never lowers your level.

Losing momentum

One busy or sick day would break my streaks, and then I'd stop opening the app.

"Take it easy" modes pause or shrink everything, and a Streak Shield power-up protects a missed day.

A crowded Today page

The more features I added, the more crowded Today got.

You choose which sections appear, and items untouched for three weeks get a gentle prompt to pause or hide them.

Weekly habits nagging daily

Weekly habits sat on Today every single day, nagging me.

"Once a week" habits hide after you do them, and any habit can be snoozed to a chosen day without losing the streak.

Too many places to look

With so many tools, I couldn't find things in my own app.

Five main notebook tabs, sub-tabs grouped by job, and one search box across the whole notebook.

Skipped reviews

I kept putting off my weekly review.

A single guided ritual reviews this week and plans the next, and Save points turn resets into "saving the game".

Blank-page habits

I had big goals but no idea what to do about them today.

Stack ideas generated from year goals, habit ideas, and a 2-minute version for every habit.

Low motivation

Some days nothing on my list felt worth starting.

"Need a lift?" picks a few quick wins from your habits, quests or dopamine-menu treats that fit.

Data worries

Everything lived in one browser, and I was scared of losing it.

Cloud sync across devices with a clear choice when copies differ, plus backups you preview before restoring.

06Features

Five tabs, like a real notebook

All screens use placeholder example data.

07Smart features

The little things I'm most proud of

Only in Questbook A kids therapy section, guided by parents

While researching, I couldn't find a single planner or habit app with anything for children. So Questbook has a section for kids' therapy routines that parents set up and guide, using the same quests and rewards that make the rest of the app fun.

  • Quick add: type "+30" after a plan to set its XP
  • A focus timer where your pet keeps you company
  • A 3D avatar whose outfit changes with the seasons
  • Each life area plays its own note as you level up
  • Letters to future you, delivered on a hard day
  • Search across the whole notebook
  • Seasonal events with bonus XP and badges
  • Draggable stationery that makes it feel like paper
08Iteration

Trying changes before they go live

For bigger changes, I built a separate preview with example data and played with it like a test build first. Only when it felt right did it go into the version I actually use. Everything on this page is the final version, with placeholder data.

Next iteration

Next: from my notebook to something people buy

Usability testingWatch new players set it up

Right now Questbook fits one person really well: me. Next I want five or six new players to try adding a habit stack, finishing a quest and redeeming a reward, so I can see where they get lost.

OnboardingYour goals, not mine

My own goals are still built into the app. Before selling it, new players need a setup that builds their own goals, life areas and rewards.

Testing the hypothesisDo people actually stick with it?

Compare how many people are still using it after two and four weeks with a plain habit tracker, and how often 2-minute versions save a streak.

Before sellingValidate the business

Interview potential buyers about what they pay for productivity apps today, and test which features make Questbook worth paying for.

Ethical designMotivating, not addictive

Keep rewards tied to real goals, never punish missed days, and let players pause without losing progress.

PrivacyJournals are personal

Be clear about where entries are stored, and let people export or delete everything.

Mentle: one calm place instead of five apps

A uni project I still care about: a phone and Apple Watch app for people who can't easily get mental health care, to help them notice their mood, handle stress and keep up good habits.

The problem

People needed four or five apps to get basic mental health support.

What I did

Interviewed people, reviewed the main apps, then designed one app that combines the best parts.

The result

A full flow, wireframes and a hi-fi prototype for phone and Apple Watch.

RoleSolo: research, UX, UI
PlatformsiOS app, Apple Watch
MethodsInterviews, competitor review
OutputFlow, wireframes, prototype
01Problem

Most people who struggle never get professional help

Waiting listsSomeone looking for a therapist may wait weeks before a first session.
StigmaSome people worry about being seen going to in-person treatment.
CostTraditional therapy is expensive, especially without insurance cover.

Talking to people, then trying every app they mentioned

I asked friends, uni colleagues and staff if they'd used mental health apps and whether they helped. Then I downloaded pretty much every app out there.

Revisited in 2026

Looking back at it in 2026

Heuristic review · Nielsen's 10Three things I'd fix first
  • Icon-only bottom bar. People have to remember what each icon means. Adding short labels supports recognition over recall.
  • Log in before any value. Someone in a stressful moment meets a sign-up screen first. Letting them try a breathing exercise before creating an account lowers that barrier.
  • No visible crisis help. A mental health app needs a "Need help now?" option on every screen, linking to local hotlines and real people.
Accessibility · WCAG 2.2White text on the brand colours is too faint
AaTeal button: 2.3 : 1, AA needs 4.5 : 1
AaCoral button: 2.1 : 1
AaDeeper teal: 5.2 : 1, passes AA and keeps the calm feel

Ratios measured from the style tile colours. I'd also check tap targets meet the 24 px minimum in WCAG 2.2.

Human-centred AIConcept: an AI journaling companion
  • Offers gentle reflections and prompts on journal entries.
  • Always says it is an AI, and never diagnoses.
  • Hands over to crisis resources and human help when an entry suggests risk.
  • Lets people see, export and delete everything it stored.
  • Evaluated by scoring sample replies for accuracy, tone and safety before launch.
Usability test planWhat I'd test next
  • 5 to 8 participants who have tried mental health apps, recruited from the same groups I first spoke to.
  • Tasks: log today's mood, start a 5-minute breathing exercise, find crisis help.
  • Measures: task success, time on task and a System Usability Scale (SUS) score.
  • Unmoderated, using a Figma prototype in a remote testing tool such as Maze.

A website for Egypt's Food Export Council

A real client project. The council connects Egyptian food exporters with buyers around the world, and they needed a site that made the industry look as trustworthy as it is.

The problem

A real client needed a site that made Egyptian food exporters look trustworthy.

What I did

Set the goals, organised the content into five sections, sketched and designed it.

The result

A full website design with a searchable product database and live chat.

MethodsGoals, sitemap, sketching

What I'd change today

Heuristic reviewMake the main task visible
  • The home page leads with a slideshow carousel. Visitors often skip carousel slides, so I'd replace it with one clear message and the product search.
  • The product database relies on several dropdown filters. I'd show active filters as removable chips so buyers always see what they've narrowed by.
